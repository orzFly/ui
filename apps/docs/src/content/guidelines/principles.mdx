## Content first, chrome quiet

**People live in these tools all day; the interface recedes so the work stays in front.**

A log, a diff, a list of cards is what someone opened the app for. Toolbars, borders and badges are
there to serve it, so they take the muted colours, the small sizes and the edges of the screen.
Give emphasis to one thing per view, the action most people came to take, and let everything else
stay calm. Every extra border, shadow or colour competes with the content for the same attention.

<Example of={QuietThread} />

A conversation from Mail. The subject and the message carry the weight; the toolbar is ghost icons
in the muted colour, and **Send** is the one filled button.

## Dense with a pointer, generous with a thumb

**One look under every pointer; a coarse one adds the room a thumb needs.**

A mouse can hit a 32px button all day, and a dense screen shows more of the work at once. A thumb
cannot, so on a coarse pointer the same components, drawn the same, take that room by themselves: a
hit area around each control, space between neighbours, rows tall enough to tap. Write one layout
and let the components make the room; do not branch the app on screen width to pass bigger sizes.
[Density](/guidelines/foundations/density/) has the sizes and when to step down from them,
[Touch & mobile](/guidelines/platforms/touch-and-mobile/) what a coarse pointer adds.

<Example of={MailSelection} />

Press **Touch** above the example: nothing is drawn larger, but the toolbar's buttons move apart
and its bar makes room for their hit areas, with no change to the code.

## Nothing only on hover

**Every action is reachable by keyboard and by touch.**

Hover is a hint, never the only way in. A row's actions are on screen, or behind a visible `⋯`
menu, rather than waiting for a hover that a keyboard and a thumb cannot give. In a control that
is one Tab stop, a tree, the `⋯` shows on the current row, the focused one and the one under the
pointer, and on every row on a touch screen, and Shift+F10 opens it. A selectable tree's checkbox
shows on a row's icon only under the pointer, but it is a shortcut: Space selects from the
keyboard, and the row's menu holds **Select** for a thumb and a pointer alike. A tooltip repeats a
label the control already carries; it never holds the only copy. Every control can be reached with
Tab and used with the keys a native control would take.

<Example of={ReachableActions} />

Tab to Mail's thread toolbar and walk it with the arrow keys: the bar is one Tab stop. Each tooltip
opens on focus as it does on hover, and repeats the button's name with its shortcut; **More
actions** opens with Enter or Space.

## A place is a link

**Whatever has an address is an `a[href]`; the app takes over a plain click and no other.**

A folder, a section, a file, an open item: anything the reader can go to has a URL, and the control
that goes there is a link to it. The browser then does the rest by itself. A middle click or
⌘/Ctrl-click opens it in a new tab, Shift-click in a new window, the context menu copies its address,
and hovering it shows where it leads. A `button` that calls the router does none of that. An app that
moves in place keeps the link and takes over only the plain left click, through its router's link
passed with `asChild` or `isPlainClick` from `@orz/ui/lib/link`.

What acts rather than goes stays a button: Archive, Save, a menu's trigger. So does picking a row in
a list box, which chooses in place, and a place held only in app state, with no address to give;
a hash (`#profile`) is often address enough to make it a link. [Navigation](/guidelines/patterns/navigation/) has the
components.

<Example of={MailFolders} />

Click a folder and it opens here, as an app's would. Middle-click it, or open it from the context
menu, and Mail opens on that folder in a new tab.

## Undo over confirm

**Confirm only what cannot be undone.**

A confirmation dialog asks a question people learn to answer without reading. For an action that
can be taken back, act at once and offer **Undo** in a toast. Keep the dialog for the few that cannot:
deleting what no one can restore, sending what cannot be unsent. Then name the thing and the
consequence, and make the destructive button say what it does.

token cannot be taken back, so Settings asks first, and names the token and what stops working.

## The theme is the user's

**No hard-coded colours; every surface reads the tokens.**

People pick a theme to read comfortably, in a dark room, in sunlight, with low vision. An app that
writes `#fff` or `text-gray-500` breaks that choice in one place and nobody notices until a user in
a dark theme does. Draw with the [colour tokens](/guidelines/foundations/color/), and let
[theming](/guidelines/foundations/theming/) do the rest.

<Example of={ThemedChecks} />

The checks of a pull request's CI run, drawn only with tokens. Pick another theme from the site's
theme menu, light or dark, and every surface, border and status colour follows.

## Say what happened

**Loading, empty, failed and done each look different and say so.**

A blank area could mean any of four things, and the user should never have to guess which. Loading
shows the shape of what is coming; empty says there is nothing yet and how to add something; failed
says what went wrong and offers a retry; done says so once, briefly, and gets out of the way. A
state that cannot be told apart from another is a bug.

## Shown, not told

**The interface says nothing it already shows; an explanation waits behind (i).**

A label, a control's state, a Save button that is there or is not: the screen already tells the
reader most of what a sentence under it would. Write that sentence anyway and every row grows a
line of grey text the reader learns to skip, taking the one line that mattered with it. So no
subtitle restates its heading, and no "Changes apply at once" sits over switches that do. What a
reader may need and cannot see, what a setting does or why an option is there, goes behind an
`InfoPopover` right after the label it explains: one press away with a mouse, a thumb or the keys.

Words that report stay on screen: an empty, failed or done message (*Say what happened*), a
field's error, and a limit the reader needs while typing. [Writing](/guidelines/content/writing/#explanations)
draws the line.
